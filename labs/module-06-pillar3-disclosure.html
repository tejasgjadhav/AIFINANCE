The RBI Pillar 3 filing set from one sentence

We take one structured data file for a real bank and turn it into the files RBI asks for: one Word document per Pillar 3 table, DF-1 to DF-18, written one after another in filing order, with a log. Then we do the part that matters. We check our ratios against the ones the bank itself published, and the run stops if they disagree.

A  build the filing set  →  B  reconcile to the bank's own numbers  →  C  peer review and AI commentary
Everything here is free, and no key is needed. Codex writes the code. Python and python-docx build the Word file. The commentary at the end can be written by pasting the ratio tables into any free AI chat. There is nothing to buy for this lab.
The data is real and it is a bank's. Every figure in the data file is ICICI Bank's own, from its Basel III Pillar 3 disclosure at 31 March 2025, consolidated, with the PDF page cited on each block: capital and RWA, NPAs and provisions, exposures by risk weight, market and operational risk capital, interest-rate risk by currency, banking-book equities, and the leverage reconciliation. Seven of the eighteen tables have no figures in the file, and the script writes those as stubs that say so. Nothing is invented. The LCR and NSFR inputs are illustrative teaching values, because the bank publishes those two ratios elsewhere, and they sit in a separate appendix file marked as such.

ABuild the filing set30 minutes

We type one sentence. Codex asks what it needs, writes a plan, and waits for approval before it builds anything.

Here is a bank's Pillar 3 data in bank_data.json, one block per RBI table. Compute CET1, Tier 1, total capital, leverage, LCR and NSFR, compare each with the RBI minimum, check them against the ratios the bank published, and then write one Word file per RBI table, DF-1 to DF-18, in filing order, with a filing log. Where the data has no figures for a table, write a stub that says so.

What the script actually does

  1. Reads the data. One JSON file, one block per RBI table, DF-1 to DF-18, each with its PDF page and a status: complete, partial, or not in data.
  2. Divides six times. CET1 over RWA. Tier 1 over RWA. Total capital over RWA. Tier 1 over total exposure. HQLA over 30-day outflows. Available over required stable funding.
  3. Builds the floor. For a D-SIB, the total capital minimum is 9.0% base plus the 2.5% conservation buffer plus the 0.2% surcharge, which is 11.7%. CET1 starts at 5.5% and Tier 1 at 7.0%, with the same buffers on top.
  4. Reconciles before it writes anything. Each computed ratio is put next to the ratio the bank published. The DF-17 rows must add to the leverage exposure. The DF-4 buckets must add to the total. One mismatch and the run stops with nothing filed.
  5. Writes one file per table, in order. 01_DF-1 to 18_DF-18, each a Word document in the RBI layout with its source pages and its data status on the first line. A table with no data is a stub that says so, not an empty table.
  6. Writes the log. 00_filing_log.csv lists every table, its status and its file. That is the checklist a compliance officer files against. A combined reading copy is written too.

A mismatch is a stop. If your total capital ratio comes out at 16.9% and the bank published 16.41%, the document is not late. It is wrong. Find the input that was mis-typed or the formula that divided by the wrong thing. A Pillar 3 disclosure that does not reconcile to the bank's own numbers is never issued.

CPeer review and AI commentary15 minutes

  1. Swap folders with the next desk. Open their 00_filing_log.csv, then their DF-2 and DF-18. Check the four ratios against the published ones without looking at their script.
  2. Paste the two ratio tables into any free AI chat. Ask for a one-paragraph management commentary on capital adequacy.
  3. Check every number in its paragraph against your tables. A wrong number, an invented trend, or a ratio it was never given is a strike.
  4. Keep only the sentences that survive. Paste them into the DF-2 file under the capital ratios. That is the AI-written part of the disclosure, and it has now been read by a person.
The rule for this hour. Automation earns its place by doing the division and the formatting. It never earns the right to skip the reconciliation, and no disclosure leaves without a human name on it.
